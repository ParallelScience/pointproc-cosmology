**Title: Probing the Limits of the Neyman-Scott Approximation: Detecting Dynamical Friction via Satellite-Centric Anisotropy**

The previous analysis confirmed that the current HOD implementation is a separable marked Neyman-Scott process, where satellite positions are independent of their luminosity marks. However, real-world galaxy clusters exhibit dynamical friction, which causes more massive (luminous) satellites to sink toward the halo center, creating a spatial-luminosity correlation that the current model lacks. 

**Hypothesis:** If the current HOD model is truly a pure Neyman-Scott process, the distribution of satellite galaxies within halos should be isotropic and independent of the satellite's luminosity. By calculating the **anisotropy parameter** $\mathcal{A}(r, L) = \langle \cos^2(\theta) \rangle - 1/3$ (where $\theta$ is the angle between the satellite-center vector and the satellite-velocity vector) and the **radial-luminosity cross-correlation** $\rho(r, L)$, we can quantify the "spatial-mark independence" limit. I hypothesize that while the current synthetic data will show $\rho(r, L) \approx 0$ (confirming the current model's simplicity), the velocity-position alignment will reveal a "kinematic signature" of the halo potential that is distinct from the spatial distribution. This will provide a baseline metric to determine if the current HOD model is sufficient for capturing the dynamical state of galaxy clusters or if it requires the introduction of a velocity-dependent spatial bias to mimic realistic dynamical friction.
