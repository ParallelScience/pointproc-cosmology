The persistent failure of the Neyman-Scott model to accurately describe the 1-halo regime across varying halo masses is not merely a result of radial profile mis-specification, but a consequence of "Stochastic Assembly Bias" induced by the discrete coupling between host halo mass and satellite luminosity. I hypothesize that the satellite-satellite correlation function $\xi_{ss}(r)$ is non-separable in mass and space because the current HOD implementation treats satellite luminosity as an independent mark, whereas the physical process of dynamical friction and subhalo disruption creates a luminosity-dependent spatial "exclusion zone" within the halo. 

To test this, I propose a "Conditional Marked-Pair Analysis": instead of using the global marked correlation function, we will compute the cross-correlation between satellite luminosity-offset marks and the local density field, partitioned by the ratio of satellite-to-central luminosity. By comparing the clustering of "bright-satellite" pairs versus "faint-satellite" pairs within the same mass-binned halos, we can quantify the spatial segregation induced by the HOD's luminosity assignment. If the spatial distribution of satellites is truly independent of their luminosity (as the current HOD assumes), the marked correlation $M(r)$ should remain unity across all luminosity-offset bins. A deviation from unity in this specific cross-correlation will provide the first empirical evidence of "hidden" assembly bias in the synthetic catalog, allowing us to calibrate a correction factor for the 1-halo spatial kernel that accounts for the discrete nature of galaxy formation in low-mass halos.
