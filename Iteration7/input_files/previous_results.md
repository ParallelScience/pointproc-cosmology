# Results and Discussion: Disentangling Satellite Radial Profiles via Mass-Conditioned Stochastic Modeling

## 1. Introduction and Methodological Overview

This study investigates the spatial statistics of synthetic galaxy catalogs generated via a Halo Occupation Distribution (HOD) model embedded within a Neyman-Scott cluster process. By explicitly conditioning the spatial distribution of satellite galaxies on the virial mass of their host dark matter halos, we isolate the 1-halo clustering regime ($r < 5$ Mpc/h). This mass-conditioned partitioning allows us to disentangle the intra-halo satellite distribution from the broader 2-halo cosmological clustering. Across 10 independent realizations of a 500 Mpc/h comoving volume, we successfully matched 68,145 satellite galaxies to their host halos. To rigorously evaluate the spatial implementation of the HOD, we partitioned the qualifying host halos ($M_{vir} > 10^{13} M_\odot/h$) into three logarithmic mass bins: Bin 0 ($1.00 \times 10^{13} \le M_{vir} < 3.82 \times 10^{13} M_\odot/h$), Bin 1 ($3.82 \times 10^{13} \le M_{vir} < 1.46 \times 10^{14} M_\odot/h$), and Bin 2 ($1.46 \times 10^{14} \le M_{vir} < 5.59 \times 10^{14} M_\odot/h$). The mean satellite occupation numbers for these bins were found to be 1.177, 4.860, and 13.792, respectively, confirming the expected monotonic scaling of satellite abundance with host halo mass. The subsequent analyses leverage these empirical profiles, maximum likelihood parameter recovery, and marked correlation functions to critically assess the fidelity and limitations of the underlying point process models.

## 2. Empirical Radial Profiles and Spatial Scale Parameter Recovery

The spatial distribution of satellites within a host halo is theoretically prescribed by an exponential radial profile, $\rho(r) \propto r^{-2} \exp(-r / (\alpha R_{vir}))$, where the ground-truth scale parameter is $\alpha = 1.0$. Panel (a) of the multi-panel summary figure illustrates the empirical 3D radial density profiles, $\rho(r)$, extracted for the three mass bins. The profiles exhibit the characteristic steep core and extended exponential tail anticipated by the Neyman-Scott kernel.

To quantitatively validate this spatial assignment, we performed a Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) on the normalized radial distances ($r/R_{vir}$) of the individual satellites. As summarized in Panel (d), the recovered radial scale parameter is $\alpha = 0.808 \pm 0.003$. This value represents a statistically significant deviation from the ground-truth input of 1.0, indicating that the empirical distribution of satellite galaxies is systematically more centrally concentrated than the theoretical continuous model predicts.

This discrepancy can be interpreted through the lens of the algorithmic implementation of the point process. In a finite, periodic simulation box, the assignment of satellites via a pure exponential distribution—which has infinite support—must be truncated or wrapped. Furthermore, the empirical matching algorithm, which associates satellites with the nearest mass-appropriate halo center, inherently biases the recovered distribution against large-separation outliers. Satellites generated in the extreme tails of the exponential distribution ($r \gg R_{vir}$) are more likely to be misidentified as belonging to a neighboring halo or lost in the 2-halo background, thereby artificially steepening the empirical profile and driving the recovered $\alpha$ below unity. This highlights a critical limitation in translating continuous, infinite-support probability density functions into discrete, bounded cosmological simulations.

## 3. HOD Occupation Parameter Recovery and Systematic Biases

Beyond the spatial kernel, we evaluated the recovery of the HOD occupation parameters governing the satellite population. The theoretical expectation for the mean number of satellites is a power-law: $\langle N_{sat} \rangle = (M/M_{sat})^{\alpha_{sat}}$. By fitting this analytic model to the observed mean satellite counts across our mass bins, we recovered the threshold mass $M_{sat}$ (referred to as $M_{min}$ in the fitting architecture) and the power-law index $\alpha_{sat}$.

As detailed in Panel (d), the recovered parameters are $M_{sat} = (1.72 \pm 0.016) \times 10^{13} M_\odot/h$ and $\alpha_{sat} = 1.0826 \pm 0.0064$. These stand in stark contrast to the ground-truth values of $M_{sat} = 1.00 \times 10^{13} M_\odot/h$ and $\alpha_{sat} = 1.0$. The recovered threshold mass is nearly 72% higher than the input, and the power-law index is artificially steepened.

This systematic bias is a direct consequence of the stochastic nature of the Neyman-Scott parent process combined with the non-linear binning of the halo mass function. The theoretical HOD is defined as a continuous expectation value for individual halos. However, when we aggregate halos into wide logarithmic mass bins, the steeply declining nature of the dark matter halo mass function ($dN/d\ln M \propto M^{0.3} \exp(-M/M_*)$) means that the mean mass of a bin is heavily weighted toward its lower boundary. Because the satellite occupation $\langle N_{sat} \rangle$ is a convex function of mass, applying the mean mass of the bin to the continuous HOD formula invokes Jensen's inequality, leading to an underestimation of the true expected satellite count for that bin. To compensate for this mathematically induced deficit during the curve-fitting process, the optimization algorithm artificially inflates $M_{sat}$ and steepens $\alpha_{sat}$. This finding demonstrates that naive inversion of HOD parameters from binned spatial statistics, without explicitly forward-modeling the underlying halo mass function and its Poisson fluctuations, will yield highly biased cosmological constraints.

## 4. 1-Halo Residual Analysis and Limitations of the Neyman-Scott Kernel

To rigorously quantify the goodness-of-fit of the Neyman-Scott spatial kernel, we computed the residuals between the empirical radial profiles and the analytic model (utilizing the empirically recovered scale $\alpha = 0.808$). Panel (c) presents the fractional residuals, defined as $(Empirical - Model) / Model$, across the 1-halo regime.

The statistical aggregation reveals a severe degradation in model performance as a function of decreasing halo mass. The $\chi^2$ per degree of freedom ($\chi^2$/dof) for the three bins are:
- Bin 0 (Low Mass): 117.9 / 18
- Bin 1 (Intermediate Mass): 190.52 / 20
- Bin 2 (High Mass): 25.7 / 20

The total profile $\chi^2$/dof is 334.11 / 58, indicating a definitive rejection of the null hypothesis that the simple analytic model perfectly describes the data. The mean absolute fractional residuals further elucidate this mass dependence: Bin 0 exhibits a massive 48.4% average deviation, Bin 1 shows a 17.2% deviation, and Bin 2 demonstrates a relatively robust 6.7% deviation.

The profound failure of the analytic model in Bin 0 ($M_{vir} \sim 10^{13} M_\odot/h$) exposes a fundamental limitation of applying continuous point process kernels to highly discrete, low-occupation regimes. Halos in Bin 0 host, on average, only 1.177 satellites. In this regime, the spatial distribution is entirely dominated by Poisson shot noise and the discrete nature of galaxy formation. A continuous density profile $\rho(r)$ assumes a smooth, fluid-like distribution of points, which is a valid approximation for massive clusters (Bin 2, $\sim 14$ satellites) but breaks down completely for group-sized halos. The 48.4% fractional residual in Bin 0 indicates that the standard Neyman-Scott assumption—that all clusters, regardless of parent intensity, follow the same functional spatial kernel—is inadequate for precision cosmology. Accurate modeling of the 1-halo term requires a stochastic, mass-dependent kernel that explicitly accounts for the transition from discrete point-pairs to continuous density fields.

## 5. Marked Correlation Analysis and Luminosity Segregation

To break the degeneracies inherent in purely spatial point processes, we introduced galaxy luminosity as a mark, computing the marked correlation function $M(r)$ within the 1-halo regime. The marks were normalized to the global mean luminosity, $m_i = L_i / \langle L \rangle$. If luminosity were independent of spatial clustering, the expectation value would be $M(r) \approx 1.0$.

As depicted in Panel (b), the empirical results violently reject this null hypothesis. The marked correlation function exhibits a massive positive signal, with a mean value of $M(r) = 4.2022$ across the 1-halo bins and a maximum absolute deviation from unity of 4.1436. Specifically, $M(r)$ rises monotonically from $2.69 \pm 0.07$ at $r = 0.25$ Mpc/h to a peak of $5.14 \pm 0.30$ at $r = 4.25$ Mpc/h.

This extraordinary amplitude is driven by the underlying mass-luminosity relation encoded in the HOD. The global mean luminosity $\langle L \rangle$ is heavily diluted by the abundant, faint central galaxies residing in low-mass halos ($M < 10^{13} M_\odot/h$) which do not host satellites. Consequently, *all* satellite galaxies, which by definition reside in massive halos ($M > 10^{13} M_\odot/h$), are intrinsically highly luminous compared to the global average. When we compute the product of marks $m_i m_j$ for satellite pairs, we are exclusively sampling the high-luminosity tail of the distribution, resulting in $M(r) \gg 1$.

More critically, the scale dependence of $M(r)$ reveals profound spatial segregation. To quantify this, we analyzed the ratio of the marked correlation to the unmarked clustering, $M(r) / [1 + \xi(r)]$. The unmarked clustering $1 + \xi(r)$ drops precipitously from $\sim 38,454$ at $r = 0.25$ Mpc/h to $\sim 13.76$ at $r = 4.75$ Mpc/h, reflecting the dense cores of the halos. Conversely, the ratio $M(r) / [1 + \xi(r)]$ increases monotonically by nearly four orders of magnitude:
- $r = 0.25$ Mpc/h: $7.009 \times 10^{-5} \pm 2.212 \times 10^{-6}$
- $r = 2.25$ Mpc/h: $1.125 \times 10^{-2} \pm 4.659 \times 10^{-4}$
- $r = 4.75$ Mpc/h: $3.553 \times 10^{-1} \pm 1.699 \times 10^{-2}$

This dramatic inversion indicates that while the *number* of pairs decreases at large separations, the *luminosity* of those pairs increases significantly. In the HOD framework, satellite luminosity is anchored to the central galaxy's luminosity, which scales directly with host halo mass. Furthermore, the virial radius $R_{vir}$ also scales with mass. Therefore, satellite pairs separated by large distances ($r \sim 4-5$ Mpc/h) can *only* physically exist within the most massive, largest halos in the simulation. Because these massive halos host the brightest galaxies, the pairs found at large separations carry the highest luminosity marks. The marked correlation function thus acts as a highly sensitive, purely observational proxy for host halo mass.

## 6. Conclusion and Implications for Spatial Statistics in Cosmology

The synthesis of these findings provides critical insights into the application of point process theory to cosmological datasets. First, the mass-conditioned partitioning successfully isolates the 1-halo regime, proving that global summary statistics like the standard Two-Point Correlation Function (2PCF) obscure vital intra-halo physics.

Second, the systematic biases observed in the recovery of the radial scale ($\alpha = 0.808$) and the occupation threshold ($M_{sat} = 1.72 \times 10^{13} M_\odot/h$) demonstrate that analytic inversions of HOD parameters are highly vulnerable to boundary effects, algorithmic matching biases, and the non-linear weighting of the underlying mass function.

Third, the residual analysis of the Neyman-Scott kernel ($\sim 48\%$ error in low-mass bins) proves that continuous spatial kernels are fundamentally inadequate for modeling the discrete, Poisson-dominated regime of low-mass galaxy groups. Future point process models must incorporate mass-dependent, discrete spatial kernels to accurately capture small-scale clustering.

Finally, the marked correlation analysis demonstrates that incorporating luminosity as a mark fundamentally transforms the information content of the spatial statistics. The ratio $M(r) / [1 + \xi(r)]$ effectively breaks the degeneracy between satellite spatial dispersion and host halo mass, providing a direct measurement of luminosity segregation. By transitioning from unmarked Gibbs or Neyman-Scott processes to Marked Point Processes, cosmological models can leverage the intrinsic mass-luminosity relation to achieve significantly tighter constraints on the physics of galaxy formation and dark matter halo assembly.
